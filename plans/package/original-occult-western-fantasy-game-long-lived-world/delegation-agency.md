# Long-Lived World — Delegation and Agency

**Task ID:** `refactor/original-occult-western-fantasy-long-lived-world`  
**Status:** Partially frozen / implementation not approved

## 1. Purpose

Delegation allows long-lived play to scale without forcing the protagonist to personally manage every case, employee, property or institution.

As scope grows, control should move from:

**direct action → named-agent delegation → departmental leadership → institutional policy**

## 2. Delegable domains

Potential delegable work includes:

- routine investigations;
- business operations;
- property management;
- research;
- travel/logistics;
- institutional administration;
- legal/financial operations;
- political/social organization;
- archive management;
- regional branches.

Not every decision must be delegable.

High-impact existential, identity, Claim or deeply personal choices may remain protagonist-only.

## 3. Agents are actors

Named agents are not scalar modifiers.

Relevant state may include:

- competence;
- loyalty;
- ambition;
- values;
- relationships;
- family;
- secrets;
- Claim/occult involvement;
- health;
- career trajectory;
- corruption risk;
- personal Agenda.

Agents age, change, retire, die, defect and may cultivate successors.

## 4. Delegation contract

A delegation should define:

- objective or responsibility;
- authority boundary;
- resource access;
- acceptable risk;
- prohibited actions;
- mandatory escalation conditions;
- reporting cadence/summary expectations.

Example:

> Grow the business conservatively, avoid church contracts, notify the protagonist on any Claim-related event, and seek approval above a major capital threshold.

## 5. Outcome model

Delegated results depend on multiple factors including:

- competence;
- information quality;
- available resources;
- loyalty/alignment;
- instruction clarity;
- institutional support;
- external environment;
- hidden conflicts or corruption.

Success is not guaranteed.

## 6. Failure and drift

Possible delegated failures include:

- poor judgment;
- hidden debt;
- fraud;
- corruption;
- loyalty drift;
- faction building;
- unauthorized deals;
- coverups;
- ideological divergence;
- outright betrayal.

Failure changes the world and may remain concealed until discovered.

## 7. Escalation

Routine decisions should remain below the interruption threshold.

Escalate when:

- authority is exceeded;
- losses cross a configured threshold;
- important actors are endangered;
- major legal exposure appears;
- Claim/occult risk emerges;
- institutional control is threatened;
- betrayal/corruption becomes material;
- a delegated matter becomes personally relevant;
- the player requested notification for that condition.

## 8. Organizational hierarchy

Large organizations may develop levels such as:

- protagonist/founder;
- executive leadership;
- regional/divisional leadership;
- local branches;
- aggregate workforce.

Only relevant nodes require individual detailed simulation.

An aggregate member may promote into a named actor if an event makes them important.

## 9. Family boundary

Practical family logistics may be delegated to partners, relatives, guardians, staff or institutions.

Delegation does not replace emotional presence.

Long absence can still alter:

- trust;
- attachment;
- resentment;
- intimacy;
- family identity.

## 10. Delegated investigations

Routine matters may be assigned to agents or an organization.

Possible results include:

- correct resolution;
- mistaken resolution;
- unresolved closure;
- hidden misconduct;
- ordinary crime;
- unexpected anomaly;
- agent injury/death;
- discovery of a Historical Hook.

If relevance rises above threshold, the matter returns to active protagonist play.

## 11. Institutional policy mode

Late-game organizations may accept durable goals such as:

- research Claim phenomena;
- expand into a region;
- avoid public occult exposure;
- prioritize family protection;
- maintain conservative financial posture;
- recruit specific expertise.

The institution then performs bounded autonomous planning under those goals.

## 12. Institutional autonomy

Player-founded organizations are historical entities, not permanent remote-controlled extensions of the protagonist.

Over decades they may develop:

- internal culture;
- traditions;
- factions;
- leadership interests;
- member loyalties;
- risk tolerance;
- political/religious identity;
- institutional memory.

Their Agenda may drift from the founder.

Extreme conflicts may lead to:

- internal resistance;
- leadership challenge;
- factional split;
- refusal to execute orders;
- attempted removal of the protagonist;
- organizational schism.
